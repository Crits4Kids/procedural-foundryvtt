> *PROCEDURAL!* is a tabletop role-playing game where players take on
> character tropes from their favorite TV crime shows and try to solve
> cases within a three-act time limit. Every game session is essentially
> an episode of an original detective show that you make up! Players
> will need to work together to not only uncover the mystery, but
>
> also compile quality evidence for a conviction. Finding out who did it
> is only half the battle-- you have to make it stick in court!
>
> If you're entirely new to tabletop role-playing games, they are
> essentially a story told between players and the gamemaster. In this
> game, the gamemaster is called the **Showrunner**. To play this game
> you will need someone to act as the Showrunner and up to four players.
> The Showrunner runs the case and brings the non-player characters
> (NPCs) to life, while the players create and play the roles of their
> character **Tropes**.
>
> Players describe what they're doing, and when a situation seems
> pivotal to the story or risky for the characters, the Showrunner might
> call for a **skill roll**. To make a skill roll, players roll two
> six-sided dice (2d6) and add them together, then add a skill modifier,
> if one applies. The dice introduce chance into the game and can make
> players consider creative new approaches to a problem. So grab some
> six-sided dice from an old dusty board game and let's get started!
>
> There are two game modes your group will choose from: **single
> episode** or **season mode**. A single episode is just that, a case
> that is played over a single game session, otherwise known as a
> one-shot. If you don't plan on playing with the same group of people,
> or play very infrequently, a single episode is the way to go. In
> season mode, the group plays out a season, solving different cases
> over multiple game sessions. Your choices and case outcomes can affect
> future cases, and your character can improve their abilities over
> time. If you are playing with the same group and want a campaign style
> game, season mode is the way to go.
>
> This game doesn't require any character prep for players, everything
> is roll-to-play! The players will need character sheets, dice, and a
> pencil. They may want to bring extra paper for notes. There is an
> optional act tracker on our site (occupiedhex.com) to aid the
> Showrunner and/or players. Players should not use phones for notes,
> mostly because phones and role-playing games are a terrible mix.

# ![](media/image11.png){width="1.1389884076990375in" height="1.926107830271216in"}MURDER, THEY MADE UP

> This game is heavily influenced by popular television crime shows. In
> real life, crimes like murder, theft, and kidnapping are decidedly NOT
> fun, but those same crimes are used in detective shows to set off a
> chain of events that include thrilling chases, clever investigative
> work, and fun character dynamics.
>
> This game contains murder (a lot of them, actually), with plenty of
> grisly details. It might not be a game for everyone, and that's ok!
> Player safety should always take precedent, so everyone should have a
> chance to express any concerns or questions they might have. Your
> group can also discuss a player safety technique for ending a scene
> that's making anyone uncomfortable, such as simply saying, "*Fade to
> black, please.*"
>
> You can always determine the tone based on the players at the table,
> but in general this game is not designed to be a gritty detective
> simulator. Instead, think of crime shows like NCIS, CSI, and any show
> where the lead detective starts to leave the room but then stops and
> says, "just one more question..."

> **STATS & SKILLS & HOW TO USE THEM**
>
> Every character Trope has their own unique **stats**, which are
> divested into their associated **skills**. When a player character
> wants to try something of consequence or with some risk involved, the
> Showrunner may call for a **skill roll**. Players then roll 2d6, add
> the results together, and if they have a modifier in that skill, they
> add that modifier to the end result.

## ![](media/image7.png){width="1.012509842519685in" height="1.3999518810148732in"}STATS

> Stat numbers are never used in skill rolls, however the points given
> to each stat during character creation are divested into their
> respective skills for a modifier that is added to skill rolls. Stats
> cover the player character's three main attributes: Mental, Physical,
> and Social.
>
> **Mental:** Your cognitive powers, used for applied sciences,
> investigating, reasoning clues, etc.
>
> **Physical:** Your physical prowess in the arts of violence, sneaking,
> or simply running away.
>
> **Social:** Your ability to convince, lie, intuit, etc.
>
> Each stat has three separate skills associated with it. Stats are not
> used in dice rolling, however the points given to each stat during
> character creation are divested into their respective skills for a
> modifier that is added to dice rolls. The higher the stat number, the
> more points you can sink into that stat's three separate skills. If a
> Trope's stat is zero, that means you don't have any points to divest
> into the related skills. In season mode, stats may be improved to show
> how your character has progressed.

## SKILLS

> Each stat has three related skills, and the stat points are divested
> into those skills during character creation. The number put into each
> skill is called a **modifier,** and this is the number added to skill
> rolls that players make throughout the game when the Showrunner calls
> for them. Some skills have a cap on them which you can only go beyond
> with certain Tropes. There are nine skills in the game:
>
> ![](media/image12.png){width="0.375in" height="0.3785553368328959in"}
> **MENTAL**
>
> **Tech:** Your ability to hack, run programs, etc. Anything related to
> computers, technology or machines. If it happens at a computer, it's
> Tech. (Maxed at 2 unless your Trope allows more)
>
> **Lab:** Your ability to analyze evidence, perform research, etc.
> Anything related to forensic science, chemistry, etc. If it's science
> it's Lab. (Maxed at 2 unless your Trope allows more)
>
> **Investigation:** Your ability to find clues or things out of place.
> Your general perception.

#### ![](media/image13.png){width="0.375in" height="0.3785553368328959in"} PHYSICAL

> **Violence:** Your ability to commit violence, implied or otherwise.
> Punching? Shooting? This covers it. Making a real intimidation threat?
> This also covers that.
>
> **Reflexes:** Your ability to stop something from happening quickly.
> Catching a punch, avoiding a crash, etc. Reflexes are always in
> reaction to something else.
>
> **Coordination:** Your ability to sneak well or run fast. This also
> covers driving in stressful situations.
>
> ![](media/image11.png){width="1.1389884076990375in"
> height="1.926107830271216in"}![](media/image14.png){width="0.375in"
> height="0.3785553368328959in"} **SOCIAL**
>
> **Cool:** Your ability to console, convince, cajole, flirt, etc. Being
> cool has many uses.
>
> **Intuition:** Your ability to discern unseen details or read
> emotions. Your gut instinct or internal lie detector.
>
> ![](media/image15.png)**Deception:** Your ability to deceive others in
> word or deed.
>
> *EXAMPLE:* Rich's character Trope gives him 3 in the Mental stat, 1 in
> the Physical stat, and 2 in the Social stat. For Mental skills, he
> puts 1 in the Tech skill and 2 in the Investigation skill. For
> Physical, he puts his single point into Coordination. And for Social,
> he puts 1 point into Cool and 1 point into Deception. During the game,
> the Showrunner asks him for an Investigation roll, so he rolls 2d6,
> adds the results together, then adds his Investigation modifier of 2.

> If a player's skill roll would have **advantage**, they may re-roll
> one or both dice and choose the result they prefer. If a player's
> skill roll would have **disadvantage**, they roll 2d6 then re-roll the
> higher of the two dice, taking the lowest result. Advantage and
> disadvantage are usually decided by the Showrunner when extra
> circumstances apply, but player Talents may also give them advantage.
> There are other ways to improve a roll, such as the **Partner Bonus**,
> which adds +1 to a roll as long as another agent in the scene has a
> modifier in the skill the player is using.

# CHARACTER CREATION

> Creating your character doesn't require any prep at all, as this game
> is entirely roll-to-play. There are many roll tables for players to
> use, but there are also options for players to make things up on their
> own, or to simply choose from a roll table. The roll tables are there
> for suggestions, speed of play and ease of use, NOT to railroad
> players into any options. At the end of character creation every
> player should be happy with their result. Here is a checklist of steps
> to create your own unique agent.

- Players roll for (or choose) a Trope, which is similar to a "character
  class" in other games. Each Trope offers its own stats and a unique
  game-bending **Talent**. Players divest their stats into the related
  skills they want, although some Tropes may specify how points are
  divested. If a player doesn't want the Trope they rolled they can roll
  again after every other player has rolled. If they still don't like
  the Trope, they may simply choose from the options remaining.

- Players roll, choose, or create a Quality and a Quirk, which further
  flavor their character.

- Players give themselves 1 Rerun Point by default, which can be spent
  to re-roll any skill roll.

- Players roll, choose, or create their **B-story**, which is a personal
  issue their character may engage with during the game.

- Players choose a second Talent from the list available.

- If playing in season mode, this is where a player may roll, choose, or
  create their unique desk item (more about this in the Season Mode
  section).

- Players roll, choose, or create their HQ, then name their agency using
  the roll tables.

- If playing with fewer than 4 human players, create NPC allies to bring
  your squad up to four agents.

# ![](media/image11.png){width="1.1389884076990375in" height="1.926107830271216in"}QUALITIES & QUIRKS

> Qualities and Quirks are characteristics every player character has
> that help to inform their character for role-playing. Players may
> roll, choose, or create their Qualities, but if they are being created
> they should be A) positive and B) limited to a word or two. Players do
> the same thing with their Quirk, except it should be something
> challenging or even negative about their character. Players may
> describe their character in the opening title sequence by showing
> their Quality and Quirk in action. Don't worry if
>
> some Qualities and Quirks are synonymous with others, it doesn't
> matter for gameplay.

# Rerun Points

> ![](media/image15.png)Sometimes things don't go according to plan, and
> that's especially true for dice rolls. If a player fails a skill roll
> and wants to try it again, they can spend 1 Rerun Point, roll again
> then choose the result they prefer! All player characters have 1 Rerun
> Point assigned during character creation. Rerun Points are a very
> limited resource so choose wisely when spending them!
>
> *EXAMPLE:* Rob fails his Lab roll to examine evidence in the lab. The
> game is in the 3rd act now and this is really important, so Rob
> decides to spend his 1 Rerun Point. He rerolls his Lab roll and gets a
> success! Well spent.

| > good at.                                     |
+------------------------------------------------+
|                                                |
+------------------------------------------------+

> Rerun Points serve an additional purpose as well: if the players find
> themselves stumped on the case, Rerun Points may also be spent to
> generate new leads. Players may pool their Rerun Points in any
> combination, spending X amount of points to obtain a new clue from the
> Showrunner, where X is the number of human players in the game. **This
> may be done in any scene, but only once per act.**
>
> Additional Rerun Points may be earned during a game session, keep
> reading. There is no limit to how many Rerun Points a character may
> hold, but they may not be given to other characters.

# GAME STRUCTURE

> *PROCEDURAL!* has more structure than most RPGs. The game is played
> out over three **acts**, with each act having four player-guided
> **scenes** within it (except Act 1, which has three player scenes
> because the Crime Scene is always the first scene). After the fourth
> scene in each act, there is an **interlude** where a player can work
> on their B-story or flashback (in season mode).

#### Act 1: The game begins! Players roll off for turn order for this act.

> **Cold Open:** The Showrunner reads the cold open supplied by the
> case. This is a brief cinematic description that kicks off the game
> and is just like a cold open in any crime show, which always leads
> directly into the...
>
> **Title Sequence:** Players take turns in turn order introducing their
> characters in the opening title montage. The player who rolled highest
> for turn order chooses the song that's playing through the title
> sequence (but anyone can hum it!) The title sequence is a quick
> snapshot of your character in action, and perhaps showcases their
> Qualities and Quirks, whether it's through one quick moment or a
> flurry of shots.
>
> **Crime Scene:** All player characters and NPC allies (if applicable)
> are present and take turns investigating one element of the Crime
> Scene, starting with Act 1's first player.
>
> **Scene 2-4:** These scenes are led by players based on turn order,
> starting with the second person in turn order.
>
> **Interlude 1:** The player who rolled lowest on the turn order is
> given first dibs on doing something in the interlude, such as working
> their B-story or a flashback. In a game with 4 human players, one
> player will not get an interlude turn in each case.

#### ![](media/image7.png){width="1.012509842519685in" height="1.3999518810148732in"}Act 2: Players again roll for turn order to begin and the highest roll establishes the first scene.

> **Scenes 1-4:** These scenes are led by players based on turn order.
>
> **Interlude 2:** The player who rolled lowest on the turn order is
> given first dibs on the interlude.

#### Act 3: The climactic third act! Players again roll turn order to begin the act, and the highest roll sets the first scene.

> This is the players' last chance to find clues and compile evidence.
> They may also, by majority vote, trigger the Arrest Phase at any time.
>
> **Briefing Phase:** If desired, the player characters go over the case
> and all of the evidence at their HQ. There are no skill rolls here.
>
> **Scenes 1-4:** These scenes are led by players based on turn order.
>
> **Arrest Phase:** If not triggered earlier, this always happens at the
> end of Act 3. This is the players' opportunity to make an arrest, or
> even several. Arrests cannot be made before the Arrest Phase, even if
> a player is sure they know who did it.
>
> **Interlude 3:** The player who rolled lowest on the turn order is
> given first dibs on the interlude.
>
> **Epilogue Phase:** The Showrunner reveals the results of the case.
> Did the suspect walk because of bad evidence? Did they turn out to be
> innocent? If playing a single episode, players can also offer an
> epilogue for their B-stories if they wish.
>
> *There is an act tracker on our site
> [(www.occupiedhex.com)](http://www.occupiedhex.com/) that features
> this structure, and players (or the Showrunner) can use it to jot down
> notes.*

## ![](media/image27.png)PLAYING EACH ACT

> Each act contains four scenes, in which the players work the case and
> gather evidence. Each scene should generally stay at one location, the
> exception being something like surveillance, where the suspect is on
> the move and players are following.
>
> At the start of each act, all players roll 2d6 for **turn order.**
> Starting with the first player, each player takes turns setting their
> scene
>
> with the location and their goal for the scene. Act 1 always starts at
> the Crime Scene, with all player characters present, and whoever
> rolled highest gets to take their action first. Every character
> chooses one activity they'd like to do at the Crime Scene (i.e.
> investigate a clue, interview a witness, etc.) before moving on to the
> next scene.
>
> When it's a player's turn to set a scene, they decide the **location**
> of the scene and their **intent** there. The Showrunner may help
> remind them by simply asking, "Where would you like to go and what
> would you like to do?" Defining location and intent are important to
> keeping the scenes efficient and meaningful. If a player is truly
> stumped about setting their scene, they can always ask the Showrunner
> to set the scene. In this case the Showrunner will set the location
> and/or the player's intent in the scene. This 19 \| *PROCEDURAL!*
>
> ![](media/image11.png){width="1.1389884076990375in"
> height="1.926107830271216in"}can be handy for new players who feel
> totally stuck but should be used sparingly as it can feel like
> railroading.
>
> Setting a scene should NOT be about **quarterbacking** (where one
> player is telling everyone what to do with their turn) or
> **metagaming** (where players are discussing knowledge or events their
> characters don't know). It's just a simple declaration of where you
> want to go and what you intend to do. This helps the player characters
> organize for the coming scene so everything can transition as smoothly
> and quickly as possible.
>
> ![](media/image15.png)When setting the scene, the player should give a
> location, the time (if applicable), and their character's intent for
> the scene.
>
> *SCENE CREATION EXAMPLES:*
>
> *"I want to go to the crime lab and analyze some evidence."* GOOD
>
> *"I want to go visit the widow at her home and ask her some
> questions."* GOOD
>
> *"I want to wait until night falls and then go stake out the office."*
> GOOD
>
> *"I want to go talk to the first witness and secretly get their
> fingerprint and then take it to the lab."* BAD-- too many locations.
>
> *"I want to go to the suspect's place of work."* BAD-- no intention
> for the scene.
>
> *"I want to interrogate the suspect and also search their car."* BAD--
> No location and more than one intention.
>
> A good rule of thumb when setting a scene is if you've said "*and*"
> more than once, you probably have too many elements to your scene.
> There is a **Briefing Phase** before Act 3 during which the players
> will be able to look over all their evidence and formulate a plan of
> action for their last crucial scenes.
>
> If the players aren't happy with the results of a scene, they can try
> again in a later scene!
>
> ![](media/image28.png)*EXAMPLE:* Brett, the Coroner, performs an
> autopsy but rolls a critical fail. They don't learn anything about the
> victim. In a later scene, Lisa sets the scene in the morgue and
> invites Brett to come along and perform another autopsy.

#### PARTNERING UP

> A good episode of a crime procedural always relies on the team working
> together, and the same is true in this game. As such, a player can

## THE SCENE OF THE CRIME

> The Crime Scene is where the game kicks into gear. The Showrunner will
> describe the Crime Scene and then the players are free to work it,
> starting with the first player. This might be talking to witnesses,
> looking for clues, examining the body, etc. **There are no Partner
> Bonuses at the Crime Scene.**
>
> The Crime Scene has already been worked by reporting law enforcement
> and will be taped off, and the site will be in the process of being
> fingerprinted by NPC techs. Any obvious evidence will have been
> collected, and the site will be in the process of being fingerprinted
> by lab techs. Any fingerprint data will be automatically available at
> the **start** of Act 2. If the player characters do not perform an
> autopsy, the autopsy/
>
> toxicology report is also automatically available at the **start** of
> Act 2. These results are basic and do not reveal anything beyond the
> base facts.
>
> Once every player character has had a turn to do something at the
> Crime Scene, that scene ends and the next scene begins. The next
> player in turn order sets the scene. \*In a game with fewer than 4
> players, keep rotating turns until there have been 4 player actions at
> the Crime Scene.

## ![](media/image7.png){width="1.012509842519685in" height="1.3999518810148732in"}LEADS & EVIDENCE

> **Leads** do what their namesake suggests, and lead the investigator
> toward more evidence or the culprit. Leads can sometimes also be
> evidence.
>
> **Evidence** is what the players gather as they work the case, and is
> tracked by both the Showrunner and (hopefully) the players. Evidence
> links the culprit to the crime. It could be physical evidence such as
> a fingerprint or item of clothing, or it could be witness testimony.
> The case notes for the Showrunner provide good and bad evidence, and
> sometimes the players will not know which is which, but the Showrunner
> always knows. Players need to gather quality evidence and not just
> rush to nab a suspect. At the end of the third act, players will
> submit the evidence they want considered in the case. If there is no
> evidence, or more bad evidence than good, it will result in an
> acquittal (which could have negative consequences in season mode).

> because they are clever rascals. When a player makes a skill roll and

## INTERROGATIONS

> Interrogations are special moments in the game when a player character
> or characters are questioning a suspect. Interrogations differ from
> questioning a witness in several ways:
>
> b The person is a suspect or person of interest in the case. b The
> player character is looking for some specific information. b The
> person is (usually) in custody.
>
> When an interrogation begins, the Showrunner secretly rolls 2d6 and
> the result is how many meaningful questions the player characters may
> ask before the suspect clams up and/or demands a lawyer. This could be
> as few as 2 and as many as 12. The Showrunner has the final say over
> what constitutes a meaningful question, but in general any question
> meant to provide information on the case is a meaningful question.
> Players should use their interrogation session wisely because they
> won't know how many questions they have!
>

> height="6.413333333333333in"}

## ![](media/image11.png){width="1.1389884076990375in" height="1.926107830271216in"}OTHER PHASES

> In addition to the scenes in each act, there are three unique phases
> in the game: Briefing, Arrest, and Epilogue.

#### Briefing Phase

> The Briefing Phase happens at the start of Act 3. It takes place at
> HQ, and all player characters are present. Players may discuss their
> plan of action going into this third and final act. They may also
> review evidence. There are no skill rolls during the Briefing Phase,
> this is just an opportunity for the players to get on the same page.
> The Showrunner has no role here, other than to prod the players to
> wrap it up.

#### Arrest Phase

> This is when the crap hits the fan. The Arrest Phase happens by
> default at the end of Act 3, however it may be triggered by player
> majority vote any time in Act 3. When the Arrest Phase begins, all
> players are present unless for some reason they don't want to be (or
> it's narratively impossible). Players may split up and arrest multiple
> suspects if they wish during the Arrest Phase, however if players
> split up the arrests are happening at the same time in-game. **The
> Arrest Phase always ends Act 3, even if it was triggered early in the
> act.**

#### Epilogue Phase

> When Act 3 ends, the Epilogue Phase begins. The players may choose
> which evidence to submit to trial. The Showrunner factors in the
> evidence and then reveals the result of the trial. If there is a tie
> for good and bad evidence, the Showrunner will roll to determine the
> outcome (odds against players, evens for).
>
> Did the players arrest the right person? Did they arrest the right
> person but only provide bad evidence so they walked? The case details
> and results come to light in the Epilogue Phase. There are no skill
> rolls in this phase, it is simply narrated by the Showrunner.

## INTERLUDES

> Interludes happen at the end of each act. During an interlude, a
> player may work on their B-story (in both modes) or have a flashback
> scene with their desk item (season only). The player who rolled
> **lowest** in turn order gets first dibs to do something in
>
> the interlude. They may choose to pass, and the opportunity moves to
> the next lowest turn order. Only one player sets a scene in an
> interlude. Once a player has used their interlude opportunity for a
> B-story or flashback, they may not use an interlude for the same
> option again until all other players have either worked theirs or
> passed.

#### WORKING THE B-STORY

> When a player chooses to work on their B-story, they are not working
> on the main case at all. Instead, they're attempting to resolve their
> character's personal issue in a single scene created by the Showrunner
> and the player. The Showrunner may build the scene by asking the
> player questions about their character or by building it on their own.
>
> ![](media/image36.jpeg){width="3.7270734908136482in"
> height="6.131666666666667in"}
>
> ![](media/image11.png){width="1.1389884076990375in"
> height="1.926107830271216in"}![](media/image15.png)*EXAMPLE:* Jen's
> B-story is that she just got a new puppy that is destroying her house.

## GETTING HURT

> If a player fails a physically risky skill roll or otherwise takes
> damage, they become **hurt!** The player checks the Hurt box on their
> character sheet. **While a player is hurt they have disadvantage on
> all Physical skill rolls and cannot use ANY skill modifiers.** This is
> bad.
>
> ![](media/image37.png)A player character may heal from being hurt
> either by spending their scene at HQ or from the Field Medic Talent.
> If a player is at HQ for their scene they may still work on the case
> while resting up. In season mode, all characters heal up at the end of
> the case.
>
> If a player character is hurt and they would get hurt again, they are
> knocked out for 1d6 hours! This ends their involvement with the
> current act, and they are up again (healed) at the start of the next
> act/phase.

> Player characters cannot die. If an injury is particularly grievous

## ![](media/image7.png)CHARACTER BEST PRACTICES

> b *PROCEDURAL!* is best played as a cooperative role-playing game.
> Some cases are tough enough to crack on their own; you don't need
> players working against each other!
>
> b It should be assumed that all information a player character learns
> is shared with other players, so they might build off that info when
> making their next decision. Some exceptions might be if a player
> character is in an area without phone service, etc.
>
> b These characters are NOT psychic! Avoid metagaming as much as
> possible. Every episode contains a briefing scene where the characters
> can discuss the case and how to close it out.
>
> b No one player is the "best" at the game. Avoid quarterbacking and
> giving unsolicited instructions to other players on what to do during
> their scene. As a group, avoid peer-pressuring a player into doing
> something they clearly don't want to do.
>
> b Remember, a role-playing game is not like a video game or board game
> that can be beaten. It's a story you tell together, and in this case a
> story like an episode of your favorite crime show. It's a story you
> tell together, and in *PROCEDURAL!* the story is like an episode of
> your favorite crime show.

## ![](media/image7.png){width="1.012509842519685in" height="1.3999518810148732in"}PLAYER SAFETY

> This is a game all about crimes, specifically murders and adult
> abductions. Players should be aware of this fact, and there should be
> a discussion beforehand wherein players can voice anything they do NOT
> want to see in the game. Players may also discuss this privately with
> the Showrunner.
>
> The tone of this game is ideally like playing a classic crime
> procedural show on a major network. It doesn't need to be edgy or
> needlessly gory, etc. Crimes such as sexual assault are not included
> in this game and we encourage you to leave them out.
>
> ![](media/image41.png)Give the players safety technology to use,
> should the game's content become too much. We suggest an easy (and
> on-theme) "Fade to black!" If things are getting too gross or
> otherwise upsetting, all a player has to say is "Fade to black" and
> the description in general stops and the game moves on with only the
> pertinent details being portrayed.
>
> *EXAMPLE:* Jen is witnessing the victim's autopsy. "The coroner makes
> an incision down the abdomen and begins removing organs. When he
> removes the stomach, he notes that it's punctured in many places,
> oozing blood and yellow bile-" Jen interrupts the Showrunner's
> description, "Fade to black, please!" The Showrunner advances the
> scene forward as the coroner is removing his rubber gloves. It seems
> the victim was fed metal shards that punctured their stomach.

## MANAGING TONE

> The tone of the game should be clear to most players, as it is similar
> to crime shows they have seen. This tone can be adjusted by how you
> portray NPCs, as well as how much you "feed" players affecting the
> tone. If a player is making wacky choices and injecting silliness in
> the game that doesn't gel with the way the other players are behaving,
>
> you can have an NPC ask them if they're alright. If a player is going
> too hardcore and making things grim and serious, you can have an NPC
> insert some levity. The B-stories may also lighten the tone
> considerably and provide a break from the murder details.
>
> There are as many different ways of playing an RPG as there are
> people, and there is no single "right" way, so be patient and enjoy
> your players' creativity.

## RUNNING A MYSTERY

> Running a game of *PROCEDURAL!* is a little different than a standard
> role-playing game.
>
> It uses the three-act structure, so there is a ticking clock for
> players. They don't have the luxury of unlimited planning and
> following up on every lead. Players love to create their own leads, so
> part of running a solid mystery is getting them back on (or near) the
> right track when they venture too far off. Make sure dead-ends are
> clearly dead-ends! Failing to solve a mystery when you weren't even
> engaged with the right chain of leads is frustrating. You also have to
> account for a player just blindly guessing and guessing right. Ideally
> the players are establishing a suspect and going to arrest them in the
> 3rd act, but there's nothing stopping them from deciding on a suspect
> right away. If this happens, remind the players that they can't try to
> arrest anyone until the 3rd Act, and they still need solid evidence
> for the trial or the suspect will walk free.

## ![](media/image11.png){width="1.1389884076990375in" height="1.926107830271216in"}RUNNING THE CAST

> The last thing you want is your own acting giving away the mystery, so
> less is more when acting as cast characters. You don't want the
> culprit to be too obvious, and if a red herring is over-the-top the
> players might smell something is off right away.
>
> If an NPC lawyers up, this should make things more difficult for the
> players. The NPC lawyers won't be cooperative with players, and they
> will probably have to find some good leverage or a good deal to get
> the suspect to talk.

## RUNNING THE ACTION

> When the NPC characters you are running (witnesses, culprits, etc.) do
> something at odds with the player characters, such as running away or
> trying to hurt them, you don't roll for these actions. Instead the
> player character affected rolls for the relevant skill. If a witness
> is running away, have the chasing player character make a Coordination
> roll to catch them. If a suspect tries to punch a player character,
> have
>
> the player make a Reflex roll to try to dodge it. This keeps the
> dice-rolling action more in the hands of your players.

## STICK TO THE TRUTH

> In many role-playing games the gamemaster can riff to their heart's
> content and improvise most or even all of the game's adventure. In
> *PROCEDURAL!* it's important to read over the case before the game
> session and stick to the important details. You may change character
> details, but the core mystery should not be changed spontaneously
> mid-game because you run the risk of your players feeling confused and
> cheated if things don't add up. Knowing what leads or evidence (or
> nothing) are in each scene is important to help you move the game
> along as efficiently as possible.

## LEGALESE

> Crime shows can often make up any legal rules they want, or bend
> science to make a cooler story. This game is no different. The golden
> rule of "The Rule of Cool" often applies here, as long as it isn't
> total nonsense or corruption on the part of the player
>
> characters. It's important to be clear that if the player characters
> break the law (planting evidence, blackmailing a witness, etc.) it
> will result in bad evidence, even if that evidence was originally
> good.
>
> Chances are, your players are not seasoned lawyers who know what is
> and isn't legal for agents to do. The last thing you want to do in the
> middle of your game is pull out your phone to search some law and
> squash the momentum. If the players are trying to do something that is
> a serious shortcut in the case (such as searching all cell tower data)
> simply tell them their organization doesn't have access to that
> technology. If they want to search a private property or sensitive
> online records, they would need a warrant, and you, the Showrunner,
> are in charge of if and when they get that warrant. Again if the
> players are attempting something that is a big shortcut in the case,
> delay the warrant a bit. If it's the third act and things are wrapping
> up, give them the warrant right away.

## B-STORIES & FLASHBACKS

> If a player is creating a B-story or flashback, make sure to help them
> create one with an identifiable challenge and potential resolution. If
> it's too unclear, the interlude could gobble up time with an extended
> scene that is looking for that moment. Interlude
>
> ![](media/image7.png){width="1.012509842519685in"
> height="1.3999518810148732in"}scenes should be somewhat brief; they
> are there for flavor.
>
> If a player is creating their own desk item, which can be used in a
> flashback, avoid items that suggest a defined outcome, such as a
> "first-place trophy".

> is familiar with is that many case details use generic descriptions
> such as "industrial area" and this allows you to paint in the detail
> of the actual neighborhood. However you may also just make up these
> neighborhoods if you prefer and keep the game in a fictional city.

## ![](media/image11.png){width="1.1389884076990375in" height="1.926107830271216in"}OUTCOMES AND RATINGS

> Each case outcome will fall into one of three categories, with the
> points they contribute to the overall case rating in ():
>
> **Successful (2):** This outcome means the players arrested the
> correct culprit and the evidence supports this for a conviction. If
> they shot and killed the culprit in the Arrest Phase (it can happen)
> there still needs to be supporting evidence.
>
> **Neutral (0):** This outcome means a mixed result. It could mean
> arresting the correct culprit but the evidence is bad, or there isn't
> any evidence. If the players at least arrested the correct culprit the
> case should receive this rating.
>
> **Unsuccessful (-1):** This outcome means an innocent person was
> arrested or shot/ killed. Innocent people are never convicted\* in the
> Epilogue Phase, regardless of evidence submitted (unlike real life!).
> *\*There is an exception to this in expansion content that will be
> outlined as such.*
>
> As the episodes go by, you will track the players' agency rating as a
> group (there's a handy Showrunner sheet for this on the Occupied Hex
> site). You may keep this rating secret from the players or tell them,
> but it's more immersive to convey the rating through the Director's
> attitude and actions. The higher the case rating, the happier the
> Director will be, obviously. As the season progresses, there are
> benchmarks for player character development.
>
> **After episode 1:** The Director reacts to the outcome (optional).
>
> **After episode 2:** The Director reacts; if the rating is less than
> 2, they may issue a warning of some sort.
>
> **After episode 3:** The Director reacts; if the rating is 6, all
> players gain 1 Rerun Point. If the rating is zero or less, the
> Director will freak out. All players may **level up** at this point
> (see next section).
>
> **After episode 4:** The Director reacts; if the rating is less than
> 4, they may issue another warning.
>
> **After episode 5:** The Director reacts; If the rating is 10, all
> players gain 1 Rerun Point. If the rating is 4 or less, the Director
> will issue an ultimatum: get it together or heads are going to roll\*.
>
> **After episode 6:** The Director reacts; the season is over. If the
> agency has a perfect rating of 12, the player characters receive a
> medal of valorous service. If you are playing another season, there is
> another level up session here.
>
> *\*Having the Director threaten to fire people or shut down the Agency
> is fun; actually doing it is less so. If the players want to play
> another season after this one, the Director can cool off a bit and
> back down from whatever ultimatum they gave, or give some
> consequences, such as moving the players' HQ to a new terrible
> location.*

## ![](media/image44.png)LEVELING PLAYERS UP

> When a player character levels up, they may choose any stat and
> improve it by 1, then divest that point into a skill, up to a maximum
> of 3 in that skill. If a skill is already at or above 3, it cannot be
> improved via leveling up.
>
> Players may also swap out their secondary Talent for a new one, but it
> cannot be a Talent that another player holds.
>
> Players may explain how their character changed or grew to explain the
> leveling up.

## MAKING & PLAYING VILLAINS

> During season mode the players may create **Villains** through their
> actions. A Villain is an NPC who was wronged by them somehow and would
> have a clear reason why they would want revenge. This could be an
> innocent person who was arrested, or even a witness who was bullied or
> threatened. It's not usually a culprit who was acquitted, because they
> are just happy to be free and probably wouldn't want to jeopardize
> that, right? You can track Villains on the Showrunner sheet. If the
> agency has 3 or more Villains, the Director will know about it and not
> be happy.
>
> A Villain's main goal is to mess with the agency however they can.
> They may show up at a Crime Scene and yell about how crooked the
> player characters are. They might have a player character's car towed
> at a terrible time. They could even show up in a B-story scene!
> **Regardless of what the Villain does, the effect gives the players
> disadvantage on all rolls in that scene.**
>
> Generally limit Villain involvement to one per episode, in one scene.
> A Villain sticks around in the game world unless a player character
> creatively resolves the issue, but using illegal tactics will likely
> just embolden them.

## ![](media/image7.png){width="1.012509842519685in" height="1.3999518810148732in"}CASES

> The case is the entire bedrock of the game session and the main
> mystery. By the end of Act 3 the case will conclude, either by an
> attempted arrest or the player characters not having a suspect to
> arrest.
>
> The case titles are for the Showrunner only and should not be shared
> with the players---they could spoil the mystery!
>
> The case provides the following info for the Showrunner:
>
> **Cold Open:** This is cinematic flavor text that sets up the game. It
> generally does NOT contain any clues for players. This is the scene
> that so many crime TV shows open with to get the audience hooked on
> the episode.
>
> **The Truth:** This is the truth of the mystery for only the
> Showrunner to know. If/when you are adding characters or locations to
> the case, make sure you don't alter the truth of the case.
>
> **The Cast:** These are descriptions of the case's main characters. In
> a TV show it would be all the big speaking parts. These are the
> characters we expect the players to come across but remember, players
> can and will do anything, so be ready to bring additional characters
> to life! You can edit characters to your liking but remember not to
> alter
>
> the truth of the case in doing so. The cast will contain the culprit
> but also often a red herring, who the players may encounter. The red
> herring character seems like the culprit but is not.
>
> **Crime Scene:** This is the description of the Crime Scene that
> players start Act 1 in. A cop or lab tech can walk the players through
> the scene and tell them basic info such as the victim's name, age,
> etc.
>
> **Autopsy & Lab Reports:** This is the autopsy report (if no player is
> the Coroner) and also the Crime Scene prints report that are
> automatically run. This is the autopsy report (if no player is the
> Coroner) and also the fingerprints report from the Crime Scene that
> was automatically processed.
>
> **Location:** This is a brief description of a location the players
> can be expected to visit.
>
> **Location Characters:** These are the characters who can be expected
> to be found at the location. Some may also be in the cast section and
> some may be bit players only mentioned here. You may add characters as
> you like but avoid overstuffing a location with characters, as it may
> confuse players and bog down the scene.
>
> **Leads:** A quality lead is underlined. This is usually the best
> thing a player can find in their scene, because it leads them further
> along on the case. A lead might not always point directly at the next
> piece of evidence, but it could point to something helpful, such as
> information the players can use as leverage against someone. Sometimes
> a lead will appear in multiple places, because there are multiple ways
> the player characters might discover it.
>
> **Evidence:** Evidence may be good or purposefully bad, such as a red
> herring or framing evidence. Evidence is **bolded** and good has a (+)
> and bad has a (-). If a lead is also evidence it will have one of
> these two symbols as well. A lead could be a quality lead but bad
> evidence, for example a workplace ID card that leads the players to
> the culprit's place of work although the ID card is for someone else.
>
> **Arrest Phase:** This tells you how the culprit will likely react
> during the Arrest Phase. Of
>
> ![](media/image11.png){width="1.1389884076990375in"
> height="1.926107830271216in"}course this is just our forecast for the
> situation, it could play out very differently in a game.
>
> The information provided in each case is dispensed in the order we see
> it most likely to be discovered, however be prepared for your players
> to go anywhere at any time! Reading over the case before you play is
> crucial to prep for the session.

## Arrest Phase

> The Arrest Phase is the climax of the episode, and should be as
> dynamic as possible. Every culprit has a unique way of reacting to an
> arrest attempt and this might result in a chase, shootout, hostage
> situation, etc. If the culprit manages to escape the players, in the
> epilogue they are picked up by law enforcement and still stand trial.
> **If this happens, add 2 pieces of bad evidence when tabulating the
> epilogue trial result.** This represents the culprit's opportunity to
> cover their tracks while they were free. It's best to not explicitly
> tell players that the culprit will be automatically caught by law
> enforcement if they botch the Arrest Phase, as it could lower the
> stakes. Also, you can alter the game as you like and if your players
> are bungling every arrest, have somebody get away clean!
